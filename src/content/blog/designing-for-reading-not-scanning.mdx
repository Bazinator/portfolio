---
title: "Designing for Reading, Not Scanning"
description: "Most product interfaces are optimized for glancing. Some of the more durable ones are built for sustained attention instead."
date: 2026-02-04
---

Many software teams describe clarity as a matter of reduction: fewer panels, fewer colors, fewer decisions on screen. That helps, but it is only part of the job. The deeper question is whether the interface supports the way a person actually needs to move through information.

Dashboards are usually built for scanning. They help someone check the state of a system, spot an anomaly, or decide where to look next. That is useful work. But a surprising number of product surfaces are not really dashboards at all. They are documents in disguise. They hold background, tradeoffs, evidence, and a sequence of decisions that make more sense when read than when skimmed.

When a surface is meant to be read, the design needs a different pace. Typography matters more than widgets. Spacing becomes an argument about emphasis. The order of the page starts doing the work that filters and badges often try to do later.

Three cues usually help:

- Keep one dominant reading path and make it obvious.
- Reserve dense metadata for the margin or a secondary rail.
- Use type size and spacing to signal what deserves slow attention.

This is one of the reasons I like working with editorial patterns inside product design. They assume that reading is not a failure mode. Sometimes it is the interaction itself.
